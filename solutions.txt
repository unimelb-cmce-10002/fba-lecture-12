Ex 2:

**1. Define decision**  
Predict risk of academic failure → affects student support.

**2. Groups**  
Domestic/international; full-time/part-time; disability status; campus location.

**3. Metric**  
Error parity (equal false negative rates) may be most important — we don’t want to miss those who need help.

**4. Slice**  
Performance might be worse for part-time students or those on smaller campuses if underrepresented.

**5. Gaps**  
Login time could penalise students with jobs. Past drop-out data may reflect unequal support in the past.

**6. Mitigation**  
Add diversity checks to training data; monitor fairness dashboards; co-design features with support teams.

Ex 3:

Who’s in Charge?

1. Ownership
The model was likely trained by the Learning Analytics team or external contractors. Responsibility for development rests with the data scientists and engineers who built the system. However, they aren’t accountable for how it’s used.

2. Process
There should be a documented appeals or review process. Currently, it’s unclear if a student can challenge a flag. Without such a process, the system risks being perceived as arbitrary or untrustworthy.

3. Oversight
Ongoing monitoring should be the responsibility of a designated team—possibly the Learning Analytics lead or a governance committee. This includes checking for drift, bias, and unintended consequences.

4. Communication
A clear explanation should be available to both students and staff, e.g., “You were flagged because your engagement dropped by 40% compared to the class average and you’ve missed two assignments.”
If no one can explain how the model works in practice, that’s a red flag.

5. Trust
Trust could be improved by:

Making the model explainable to non-experts

Creating opt-out policies for students

Publishing impact reports or audit summaries

Ensuring transparency about what data is used and how

Summary Judgement

Responsibility for building and training the model lies with technical staff.
Accountability for its use and impact should rest with senior leadership (e.g., Director of Student Success or equivalent).
A lack of clear oversight or appeal mechanisms would undermine both student trust and system credibility.

| **Intervention Design** | **Classification** | **Reason** |
|--------------------------|---------------------|------------|
| Pop-up Alert | Ethical Nudge | A timely, actionable message that gives the student agency. It informs without pressure. |
| Progress Bar | Borderline / Context-dependent | Could be ethical if used to motivate and inform. But if colors like red/green trigger anxiety or shame, it risks coercion. Depends on tone and framing. |
| Default Booking | Manipulative | Automatically scheduling appointments without explicit consent reduces agency. This is a classic "default effect" used coercively. |
| Peer Ranking | Manipulative | Public or comparative rankings may induce social pressure, shame, or competition—especially in vulnerable students. Risks outweigh benefits. |
| Motivational Quote | Ethical Nudge | Harmless and unobtrusive. A light motivational nudge that encourages action without manipulating. |
